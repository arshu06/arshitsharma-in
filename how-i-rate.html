How I rateOne question per dish, and the odds you order well

The Reorder

Every dish I eat gets one question. Would I order that again? Yes or no. Not whether it was clever, not whether it photographed well, not whether the chef meant something by it. Would I spend another meal on it.

The restaurant's rating is the answer across the whole table. Four dishes, two I'd have again, that is 2 in 4.

Everything on the table gets asked the same question. Starters, mains, sides, desserts, drinks. If it came off that menu and I paid for it, it counts. It does not have to have been my own order, only something I ate enough of to have a view on.

That number is the odds you order well if you walk in and pick something. It is the thing I would actually want to know before sitting down, and no rating system in food has ever told me.

Why odds and not a percentage

Saying seventy-five per cent when I ate four dishes pretends to a precision I do not have. 3 in 4 is simply what happened. When I go back and eat five more, it becomes 6 in 9, and you can see exactly how much eating the number is built on.

Why this works everywhere

Money is not universal. You cannot compare a ₹40 plate of momos to a ₹3,000 tasting menu on price, and rating both out of ten produces a number that means two different things.

Dishes are universal. Two plates at a roadside dhaba, twelve courses in a dining room, one steel thali at a wedding. Every one of them puts food in front of you, and every one of them can be asked the same question with the same meaning.

Would I go back?

Yes or no, printed on every review. No hedging, no "maybe if I was passing". It is the only judgement I make that cannot be argued down by a number, and it is the one most people actually want.

The thirty

Underneath the Reorder sits a score out of thirty. Food out of ten, value out of ten, service out of five, room out of five. Whole numbers, no halves.

Food · 9–10 best in its category · 7–8 clearly above the norm · 5–6 competent and forgettable · 3–4 noticeably off · 1–2 bad

Value · 9–10 would have paid more · 7–8 the bill never crossed my mind · 5–6 priced about right · 3–4 I felt the bill · 1–2 robbed

Service · 5 attentive without hovering · 4 smooth · 3 functional · 2 slow or absent · 1 bad

Room · 5 would sit two hours · 4 comfortable · 3 fine · 2 too loud, bright or cramped · 1 unpleasant

The Reorder is the table. The thirty is the whole evening. They are supposed to disagree, and when they do, the gap between them is the review. A kitchen can produce two dishes I would happily eat again on a night that was still the worst of the year, because of a waiter who would not look at me and four separate requests for water. One number could never say that. Two can.

What I am not

I am not a chef and I have never worked in a kitchen. I have no formal training in food, and reading about a technique is not the same as knowing it. I hold that maggi between two slices of grilled bread is a genuinely good sandwich.

What I can tell you is whether the thing in front of me was worth eating, worth paying for, and whether I would order it a second time. That is the entire promise here, and I try not to claim anything past it.

Why I can say a place was bad

Almost every food account in Tricity sits inside the local restaurant economy. They know the owners, they want the invitations, they need those relationships to keep working. Which is precisely why so few of them will tell you a restaurant was not worth the money.

I am not in that economy. I live nearly two hundred kilometres away and I am driving home on Sunday. Nobody here knows my family.

That is not a virtue. It is a fact about my situation, and it is the reason the scores on this site can say what they say.

Three rules

A dish I have no view on does not count. If I did not eat enough of something to judge it, it is skipped entirely, in or out of the total. Padding the denominator would be as dishonest as padding the score. Not finishing something is a different matter: if I stopped because I did not like it, that is a view, and it counts as a no.

Coke Zero does not count. I order one everywhere I go, which makes it a control rather than a dish. It stays out of the maths and in the writing. Every other drink is scored like anything else on the table.

I pay for my own meals. If one is ever comped or discounted it is marked at the top of that review, every time, without exception. No review here has needed that mark so far. I do not take money to write about a restaurant and I do not take money to remove one.

Corrections get published, not buried. If I have got a dish name wrong, a price wrong, or a cuisine wrong, I fix it in the open and say what changed.

Scores are dated

Every review carries the date it went up, and every judgement inside it is fixed to that date. When a review calls something the best or the worst I have had, it means as of then. I do not go back and quietly adjust an old review when a new meal beats it, and I do not print a running count of how many places I have been, because that number is wrong the moment I eat somewhere else. The date is what tells you how much eating a claim is standing on.
